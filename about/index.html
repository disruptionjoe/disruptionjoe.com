About Joe

I’ve never followed a conventional path.

I’ve founded organizations, helped build startups, led growth teams, designed governance systems, and worked with organizations trying to figure out what to do with AI.

At Gitcoin, I helped grow a platform from roughly 10,000 to 100,000 users, then went on to build its fraud detection and defense program, helping prevent more than $3 million in fraudulent funding.

I’ve designed and helped lead governance and funding initiatives across decentralized organizations, including programs responsible for distributing millions of dollars in grants and coordinating decisions involving far greater resources.

I’ve also helped build communities, organized conferences bringing together thousands of people, and worked across product, operations, strategy, and organizational transformation.

My career hasn’t followed a conventional ladder.

Before much of that, I was a DJ. I’ve worked with early-stage founders, corporate executives, software engineers, researchers, and people trying to coordinate enormous decentralized ecosystems.

I’ve been responsible for making ambitious initiatives work, not just advising from the sidelines.

And somewhere along the way, I discovered that the variety of my experience was becoming one of my greatest strengths.

The more different problems I encountered, the more I started recognizing the same patterns.

The experience that changed how I think.

Around 2018, I participated in a Google Design Sprint for Chicago’s CityKey initiative.

I’d been in plenty of meetings and workshops before. I’d also spent years speaking in public, organizing events, and getting comfortable leading a room.

But this experience was different.

People who hadn’t worked together before were collaborating effectively. Everyone contributed. The conversations moved with purpose. And the group was making real progress toward a shared outcome.

The facilitator wasn’t just managing who got to speak. The process itself was designed to produce something useful.

I was fascinated.

I started experimenting with those techniques in my community work, then in startups, consulting engagements, and increasingly complicated organizational initiatives.

What intrigued me most was how much unnecessary time was built into conventional workshop formats.

An exercise prescribed for forty minutes might deliver most of its value in five. A process designed for an afternoon could sometimes accomplish its essential purpose in a fraction of that time.

I began stripping exercises down to what actually made them work, combining techniques, adjusting the timing, and learning when to abandon the original plan because the group needed something different.

I’ve been experimenting with that ever since.

And somewhere along the way, I realized I loved facilitating these conversations even more than being the person speaking to the room.

I’m naturally more introverted than people might expect from someone who’s been a DJ and public speaker. I know how much valuable thinking can stay inside someone’s head when a meeting rewards whoever speaks first or loudest.

Good facilitation changes that.

It gives everyone a way to contribute, and it makes those contributions useful to the group.

Different problems. The same underlying process.

One of my most important realizations came while working at Gitcoin.

I’d been leading data-driven growth, helping the team understand how to attract users, improve onboarding, and increase engagement.

Then the platform began facing increasingly sophisticated fraud and Sybil attacks.

I founded and led our fraud detection and defense efforts. We developed ways to identify suspicious activity, evaluate thousands of grants and applications, and protect funding intended for legitimate projects.

On the surface, growth and fraud prevention had almost nothing in common.

One was about helping legitimate people use a product. The other was about identifying people trying to manipulate it.

But I found myself approaching both problems in much the same way.

Get clear on what’s actually happening. Challenge how the problem has been framed. Bring together people who understand different parts of the situation. Identify what success would look like before falling in love with a solution.

Generate alternatives, compare them, make a decision, and establish when to evaluate the results.

Then do it again, a little faster and a little better.

I saw that pattern again in decentralized governance.

At Arbitrum, through Plurality Labs, I helped design and operate an ecosystem of funding programs that supported hundreds of grants, using different mechanisms to help communities decide how resources should be allocated.

In other governance and organizational initiatives, I’ve helped stakeholders work through consequential decisions, coordinate competing interests, and turn broad ambitions into operating systems that people could actually use.

The stakes, people, and subject matter change. The underlying challenge is remarkably consistent.

Organizations have enormous amounts of knowledge and experience distributed across their people. Their ability to make progress depends on how effectively they put that collective intelligence to work.

That’s why I care so much about structure.

I learned in startups that speed matters, but moving quickly without shared goals, clear responsibilities, and good decisions can send talented people in different directions.

The right structure doesn’t constrain people. It gives them the clarity and trust to act independently.

And the same thing is true of a conversation.

A well-designed meeting can produce the understanding, decisions, and commitments that unlock weeks or months of productive work.

An unstructured one can consume everyone’s time and leave them exactly where they started.

Once you’ve experienced the difference, it’s hard to accept the old way.

Building a life around what I love.

For a long time, I built my career around being useful in a wide range of roles.

I joined ambitious startups. I founded and led initiatives. I took responsibility for complicated engagements involving strategy, operations, technology, governance, and implementation.

I’m proud of that work, and of the outcomes I helped make possible.

But I also found myself spending a lot of time on work I was capable of doing without particularly loving it.

And I kept noticing that the parts I looked forward to most were also the parts people most consistently thanked me for.

Meeting unfamiliar people. Learning how their organizations worked. Recognizing the question nobody was asking. Bringing a group together and helping them get somewhere meaningful.

Years ago, when I was organizing Chicago Blockchain Project, we held regular community consulting sessions. I met with more than a hundred startups, listened to what they were building, and helped them think through their challenges.

I loved those conversations.

I still get the same satisfaction talking to someone I’ve just met at a conference, hearing about their business, and helping them see a problem or possibility differently.

It’s the feeling that comes when someone says, “That was really helpful,” and you can tell they mean it.

I’ve been thinking a lot about Ikigai, the Japanese concept of finding purpose in the things that make life worth living.

For me, the question became pretty simple.

Why wouldn’t I build my professional life around the work I love most, believe I’m best at, and know other people find valuable?

So that’s what I’ve decided to do.

I want to become exceptionally good at two things:

Finding the conversation that needs to happen.

Making that conversation extraordinarily productive.

I’m building a solo practice around those capabilities, one conversation at a time.

I want to work with many different organizations because every unfamiliar problem, team, and industry gives me another opportunity to sharpen my judgment and improve my craft.

I want the quality of my work to create my reputation, and that reputation to give me the freedom to spend more time doing work I love, including pursuing my own creative projects and ideas.

I don’t need to build a large consulting agency or manage a hundred people.

I’d rather become someone people know they can call when their team needs to make real progress.

And I don’t want you to have to decide whether I’m worth hiring based on my résumé, my credentials, or a polished consulting proposal.

I’d rather you experience what I can do.

If that first conversation is valuable enough that you want another, I’ve earned it.

That’s the professional life I’ve decided to build.

And that’s what Thinking Better Together means to me.
