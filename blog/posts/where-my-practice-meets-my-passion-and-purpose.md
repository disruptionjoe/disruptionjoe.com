### Why I'm Building Disruption Joe's AI Activations Playbook

I've spent most of my life at the intersection of people, systems, and decisions.

I didn't always call it that.

In my twenties, experiences in sales and the automotive industry gave way to bartending and DJing as I learned who I was. That taught me how to read a room and hold attention. How to make people feel included. How to move energy without forcing it.

Then it became startups, local community groups, my own businesses, and high-stakes decision-making. From customizing CRM systems at independent auto dealers to guiding eight-figure budgets through decentralized governance, I've seen collective decisions at every scale.

Whether it is a room where millions of dollars are being allocated or a room deciding on HOA fees for next year, it looks the same to me. People are trying to figure out what to do next, often without a clear and legitimate way to do it together.

Decisions are the machinery behind organizations. Legitimacy is the glue.

**How we organize the people and systems to make decisions is what separates success from failure.**

Somewhere along the way, I fell in love with facilitation.

The real kind. Structured conversations that help groups surface what matters and move forward. Sessions where people leave seeing a problem differently than when they walked in.

I love the air of creativity when introverts get to say as much as extroverts. I love "aha" moments and the recognition that none of us have all the answers, but all of us have something to add.

But most meetings don't feel like this.

Small or large, most groups don't have a thinking problem. They have a thinking-together problem.

## The moment I saw the system break

One of the clearest moments came when a company I had started was acquired shortly after I landed a large contract.

We had a shared vision of a world where anyone anywhere could earn the value they are capable of creating. The key was to allow open participation, better review systems, and verifiable commitments that enforced accountability. It was infrastructure that could make governance less corrupt, more accessible, and more effective.

At the same time, there was real revenue on the table from a different direction: tooling for milestone-based grant payments.

Nobody made a bad decision. The pivot was rational. The incentives simply pulled the work away from the deeper problem, which was the one that actually mattered to me.

**Financing came for short-term returns, but the world needs long-term solutions.**

I'd written about that incentive problem before the acquisition and long before the pivot. It was one reason I landed a $3 million contract in the first place.

I could see the iceberg ahead clearly. But I was not steering the ship. And the ship was not really being steered by a person anymore. It was being pulled by the incentives around it: toward the nearest revenue, the clearest return, and the path that looked safest until it was too late.

The problem for me isn't that the project didn't succeed. The problem is that we were so close to solving a problem for the world that simply needed more time to incubate.

That is the thing about systems. They don't just reflect vision. They reflect incentives. And incentives are incredibly hard to fight from the inside.

## The moment I saw what actually works

On the other end of the spectrum, I've seen what happens when you get it right.

I worked on a cross-functional effort with the Chicago City Clerk's office, exploring identity systems and whether blockchain could play a role.

**What stood out had nothing to do with the technology. It was the room.**

Without structure, it would have become what most of these conversations become: a few loud voices, a lot of people holding back, and a general sense of "this probably won't go anywhere anyway."

Government settings are famous for participation theater.

But with the right facilitation, something different happened. People who normally wouldn't speak contributed real insights. Different departments surfaced constraints that were invisible before. Technologists, operators, and policymakers started to actually see one another's perspectives.

The problem didn't get simpler, but it got clearer.

Even in stubborn environments, where the barriers were obvious, bringing out the right voices in the right way let us see that those barriers were manageable.

That is the shift I care about.

## The same pattern, again

I saw it again in a grants organization run by volunteers that was doing great work but struggling to function.

Some people felt overworked. Others felt like they had never agreed to take on so much responsibility. Tension was building, but none of it was being expressed in a way that could actually be resolved.

Through a series of structured sessions, we created space for people to say what they were really thinking without blame. We then rewired implicit expectations into explicit agreements.

And once that happened, something unlocked. Nobody imposed a solution. The group simply saw the problem clearly, together.

That is the pattern.

When people are included, when perspectives are surfaced, and when thinking is structured, groups make better decisions.

Over the last 15 years, I've leaned on my passion for facilitation as the hidden through-line in my work. I've studied Google's design sprint methodology, worked through AJ&Smart exercises, and reverse-engineered what makes Liberating Structures and Atlassian's playbooks so durable.

The pattern is simple:

**Structure the thinking, include the people, and the decisions get better.**

## Where AI comes in

AI is entering every conversation.

There is a wave of people selling prompts without depth. There is a heavy focus on automating admin work and cutting costs.

**The dominant story is AI as efficiency. The story I care about is AI as better thinking.**

AI helps people clarify their thinking, explore ideas more broadly, and notice angles they would have missed on their own. In addition to doing more, it helps people do things they could not do before.

If you follow that path far enough, something clicks.

You start to leverage your time the way capital gets leveraged in finance. You begin thinking at a different level and seeing possibilities that were not there before.

That is the unlock.

But most people never reach it alone. They need to experience it.

Companies are going to automate workflows and cut roles. That is real. But that is not the whole story. They still need to create more value.

**I believe the organizations that help individuals reach their personal "aha" moment with AI will be the bigger winners of the next decade.**

Maybe it is because I was early in using AI. I was working with machine learning and natural language processing for fraud detection in 2022 and led an AI agents marketplace standards project in early 2025.

Maybe it is because I genuinely love walking people into an "aha" moment and showing how well-structured facilitation can create clarity and momentum that would not exist otherwise.

Whatever it is, I feel compelled to build my practice at the intersection of those experiences.

Most of all, I want to build a future where my practice is coherently aligned with my passion and purpose.

## Why I'm doing this

My passion is helping people think better together.

I love designing the room before anyone enters it: thinking through what a specific group needs, what will unlock them, and how to create conditions where the work feels lighter, clearer, and more alive.

I live for what happens in the room when it lands. Quiet people start contributing. The energy shifts. Chaos begins to find shape. The weight of ambiguity lifts and people leave with their brains buzzing, ready to move.

**I design the conditions where people become personally activated by AI and by each other.**

A normal AI workshop gives people examples. My sessions give people an experience.

A normal workshop says, "Here's what AI can do." My sessions help people **feel the possibilities** and think, "Oh wow. I can think, work, and contribute differently now."

That is what I design for: an experience where people discover what AI, structure, and better collaboration can make possible for them.

This practice exists because this work sits at the intersection of what I love, what I'm good at, and what I believe matters.
